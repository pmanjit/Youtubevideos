# SCRIPT — cold_snap
POV: Second-person · Target 930 words · ~5:00

---

The cold is not what kills you.

You will learn that later — at the first door you put your shoulder into, when the person on the other side is already holding something heavy.

Right now it is dark, your breath is hanging in the air inside your own apartment, and out past the frost on your window, three people are standing in the snow, doing exactly the math you are doing.

How much food is left. How many doors are still shut. How long until the pipes freeze.

Here is the first thing nobody tells you about a sudden drop.

The temperature does not kill people. It negotiates.

At thirty-five degrees core, your hands stop working. Zips, knots, matches — all of it gone.

At thirty-four, your judgement goes before your body does. You start making confident, wrong decisions.

At thirty-two, you feel warm. That is not recovery. That is your blood vessels opening and dumping heat into your skin so fast that your last clear thought is to take your coat off and lie down in the snow.

So the cold does not need to be dramatic. It just makes you agreeable.

Which means you have a window. Maybe seventy-two hours where you are still the most capable person in the building.

Use it or waste it. Those are the options.

Rule one. Water beats warmth.

Snow is not water. It is roughly one part water to nine parts air, and eating it cools your core while it leaves you thirstier.

So you melt it. Every hour. Dehydration is quieter than hypothermia and it always arrives first — your blood thickens, your hands go cold for reasons that have nothing to do with the room, and your heart starts working against you.

Fill every container you own. That water is also your cleaning supply, your cooking reserve, and the only thing that buys you time if you get hurt.

Rule two. Heat is a room, not a fire.

You cannot heat an apartment. You can heat one room inside it, and then you seal it. Blankets over the doorways. The smallest room, with the fewest exterior walls. Every body you trust, inside it.

One adult at rest throws off about the heat of a hundred-watt bulb. Two people in a sealed room is not twice as warm. It is better than twice, because you stop losing what you already have.

Rule three. Count calories, not cans.

In the cold, your body spends three to four thousand calories a day just staying at temperature. Most people own less than three days of that.

So tonight, do the arithmetic out loud. Calories divided by thirty-five hundred. That number is your countdown. Not the weather. Not the news. Your countdown.

And now the part you have been avoiding.

The cold is patient. People are not.

In the first three days, everyone cooperates. Neighbours share. Strangers help. That is real, and you should take it, because those days are when the lasting bonds get made.

Then the shelves empty. And the same people who helped you are solving a different problem, with your supplies in the equation.

They are not monsters. That is the thing you have to get right. Your neighbour is not a villain. Your neighbour is a parent with a hungry kid and a plan, and the plan is arithmetic, and your name is in it.

You cannot spot them by looking. The dangerous one has a good story, a calm voice, and a reason to be at your door.

So you build rules before you need them.

Rule four. Never let a stranger see your supply.

Smoke is a signal. Footprints are a map. The smell of cooking is an advertisement.

Burn dry, and burn in daylight, never at dusk when the column reads as a beacon.

Scavenge out and come back by different routes. Never let anyone walk you home.

Rule five. Meet at the boundary, not the door.

When someone knocks — and someone will — talk through the wall. Trade at the threshold. One item out, one item in, hands where you can see them, no entry.

The doorway is a negotiation. Not a courtesy.

And rule six, the one that keeps you alive past week two. Team up. But slowly.

Alone, you cannot sleep and watch and scavenge and heal at the same time. Solo survival fails on the second night, when your body finally forces you under and nobody is watching the window.

So you want people. Just not people who picked you at random.

Take one at a time. Meet away from where you sleep. Agree the terms before you need them — who watches, who cooks, what happens when someone gets sick, what happens when someone wants to leave.

And split the cache. Never one pile, never one hiding place, never one person who knows all of it. Trust is not the absence of a plan. Trust is a plan that still works if the person breaks it.

That is the whole thing, really.

The cold is a clock. It tells you how many days you have.

People are the weather inside those days. And the difference between dying in week one and reaching spring is not a warmer coat.

It is who is standing next to you when the countdown runs out.

So go to the window. Count the figures in the snow. Then go back inside and start deciding, because the cold will not wait for you, and the people outside already stopped waiting.

Extend a hand if you have to. But only once you know what the other hand is holding.
